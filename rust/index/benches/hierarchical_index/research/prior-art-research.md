# Prior art for a hierarchical SPANN white paper

This note assesses positioning against primary sources, not the novelty of Chroma's specific implementation. A useful industry paper does not require a new maximum dataset size, but a claim that hierarchical SPANN itself is new conflicts with published work. The strongest comparisons are maintained centroid trees in turbopuffer and CockroachDB, followed by the original SPANN and SPFresh papers. Research date: October 3, 2026.

## Distinguish three meanings of hierarchy

A hierarchy can organize construction, route a query through stored levels, or distribute work across machines. These are different claims. A clustering algorithm can recursively divide training data while producing an index whose searchable head is a graph. A persistent centroid tree instead stores centroids as entries in parent partitions and traverses these levels during search. Distributed routing adds a separate question: which machines receive each query. A paper should specify which of these structures it implements and how updates preserve that structure.

## Direct predecessors

**The original SPANN paper already uses hierarchy in construction and distributed routing.** The single-machine design keeps a centroid index in memory and vector posting lists on disk; hierarchical balanced clustering builds the partitions. Section 4.3 also evaluates a distributed extension using balanced partitions, replicated boundary vectors, selective machine dispatch, and workload-aware packing. The experiments use SPACEV1B and up to 32 machines. The conclusion explicitly calls this “hierarchical SPANN.” This does not establish the same arbitrary-depth, recursively maintained on-storage structure as Chroma's, but it rules out saying that hierarchical or sharded SPANN has never been published. The paper also acknowledges GPU-accelerated index construction. [SPANN, NeurIPS 2021, especially §§3 and 4.3](https://proceedings.nips.cc/paper/2021/file/299dc35e747eb77177d9cea10a802da2-Paper.pdf)

**SPFresh establishes local maintenance of vector partitions.** Its protocol inserts and deletes vectors, splits oversized partitions, merges undersized partitions, and reassigns vectors near changed boundaries. Section 3.1 describes the inherited searchable centroid head as an in-memory SPTAG graph. Section 3.3 derives necessary conditions for reassignment, then limits practical checking to nearby partitions. This is important when comparing guarantees: mathematical conditions and an approximate neighborhood search are different parts of the method. Novelty cannot rest on local splits, merges, or reassignment alone. The venue is SOSP 2023; the accessible arXiv posting is dated 2024. [SPFresh, §§3.1–3.4](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.14452v1)

**CockroachDB describes a recursively stored and maintained centroid tree.** Its C-SPANN design recursively clusters centroids into parent partitions, stores partitions in its key-value layer, and searches downward from the root through promising partitions. The article describes incremental balanced splits, movement of vectors between neighboring partitions, and removal of undersized partitions. It also describes local quantization relative to a partition centroid. This is direct conceptual prior art for a persistent multilevel SPANN-family index, rather than merely a hierarchical training algorithm. A qualification matters: the June 23, 2025 article's future-work section says merges and partition reassignment are not fully implemented in that release. The article's title mentions billions, but the described tree-depth arithmetic should not be presented as a controlled billion-vector benchmark. [Cockroach Labs technical article](https://www.cockroachlabs.com/blog/cspann-real-time-indexing-billions-vectors/)

**Turbopuffer explicitly describes a nested SPFresh-derived centroid tree.** Its ANN v3 article says it extends the original graph head by nesting clusters, uses roughly 100 children per node, and controls the number of clusters searched at each level. The hierarchy limits dependent cold object-storage fetch rounds; the large-scale deployment keeps the tree on SSD. Its system combines hierarchy with binary quantization, reranking, processor optimizations, and distribution. Therefore, its reported 100-billion-vector result is a system result, not evidence that hierarchy alone yields that performance. Chroma can publish a narrower contribution without reproducing that scale, but cannot characterize a recursive SPFresh tree as absent from public industry descriptions. [Turbopuffer ANN v3, updated May 5, 2026](https://turbopuffer.com/blog/ann-v3)

## Broader background and future work

**Hierarchical clustering trees for nearest-neighbor search predate SPANN.** The FLANN paper describes hierarchical k-means trees and search over their branches. It is background for the routing idea, although it does not by itself establish the storage and maintenance behavior relevant to Chroma. [Muja and Lowe, Scalable Nearest Neighbor Algorithms for High Dimensional Data, 2014](https://www.cs.ubc.ca/research/flann/uploads/FLANN/flann_pami2014.pdf)

**GPU acceleration has related prior art and needs a specific claim.** FusionANNS combines CPUs, a GPU, and SSD storage for billion-scale search, using multiple index tiers, selective reranking, and redundant-I/O removal. Its reported improvements compare with SPANN and RUMMY. This makes GPU support a reasonable extension, but adding a GPU is not independently a novelty argument. [FusionANNS, FAST 2025](https://www.usenix.org/conference/fast25/presentation/tian-bing)

## Implications for the paper

The strongest plausible contribution is a precise algorithm and reproducible account of how a recursive index remains correct and useful as data changes. This is a positioning recommendation, not a verified novelty finding. The specific differentiator must be identified from Chroma's implementation and compared with the prior work above. Candidates include a maintenance protocol with explicit invariants, handling of structural changes across levels, a quantified memory-versus-recall tradeoff, or evidence about when recursive routing outperforms a graph head.

A useful evaluation should test the causal claim at an affordable scale. Compare recursive routing with the same system's flat or graph-backed routing at equal recall, using identical data, storage, and compression. Measure query latency, memory, reads and bytes, insertion cost, and quality after sustained updates. Include multiple tree depths and a workload that shifts the data distribution. Report cold and warm cache conditions separately. A smaller controlled experiment can establish these properties; it cannot establish 100-billion-vector production performance.

GPU execution and sharding should remain future work unless they are necessary to validate the paper's central claim. Neither is an empty field, and neither substitutes for isolating what the current algorithm contributes. Avoid “first hierarchical SPANN,” “first recursive centroid tree,” and unsupported extrapolation from tree height to end-to-end latency. A more defensible framing is a documented implementation and evaluation of a particular maintenance and routing design, with its inherited components named explicitly.

## Benchmark evidence reviewed on October 3, 2026

The supplied benchmark collection materially strengthens the publication case. It contains large-index measurements and experiments organized around cluster sizes, datasets, dimensions, distance functions, reassignment precision, replication, thread scaling, write search width, and routing precision. The index links a pinned benchmark revision. These are existing research assets, although the presence of a child page alone does not establish the quality of its experiment. [Benchmark collection](https://app.notion.com/p/34458a6d8191806db1def23e20c13d77)

The MS MARCO page records a completed 113.52M-vector index and a four-level search evaluated with 100 queries at k=100. At tau 2 and an eightfold final candidate rerank, it reports 92.98% recall@100 with average latency of 79.7 ms warm and 259.1 ms in its cold pass. Its cold definition involves lazy posting and embedding loads; these are not p99 service latencies or a verified cold object-storage test. The run reports Brute-force GT: false, so the provenance and corpus alignment of the loaded ground truth should be documented before publication. Resumed build sections change code versions and report cumulative index size alongside partial-session elapsed time; their printed overall rates must not be treated as complete end-to-end construction rates. [113M MS MARCO](https://app.notion.com/p/34658a6d819180a89c9fff44ddc8a1a5)

The Wikipedia page records 40M vectors and a four-level tree, with precision and reranking sweeps. One compressed-routing row reports 95.92% recall@100 and 49.8 ms average latency. The recorded build summary also reports 579 orphaned IDs; the meaning and current status of that diagnostic need to be resolved before using the run as correctness evidence. [40M Wikipedia](https://app.notion.com/p/34458a6d8191808b8f9ed1330132c1b0)

The chat titled “Index Memory Optimizations” (thread ID 01a0d6c5-eb50-7d21-889a-359926993a01) reports comparable 1M-vector build measurements on the same EC2 machine, using ten 100K checkpoints and full-precision writing. Wikipedia improves from approximately 259 to 68 seconds (3.8 times faster), and MS MARCO from approximately 403 to 139 seconds (2.9 times faster). The included command outputs give candidate repeats of 66.13/69.96 seconds and 136.24/141.76 seconds respectively. This compares implementation revisions, not other indexing algorithms. The chat explicitly qualifies that these measurements precede the exact merged combination and that two repeats do not establish recall equivalence. A controlled replay attributes the main gain to avoiding repeated persisted-version reads during balancing, with approximately 2 MiB of dense-ID version cache per million vectors. [Version-cache change](https://github.com/chroma-core/chroma/pull/7842)

The recommended publication framing is therefore a concrete implementation and empirical study of an incrementally maintained hierarchical index, evaluated at more than 100M vectors, with separately scoped build-optimization results at 1M. Do not combine these into a claim of a 3–4 times speedup at 113M. Before publication, pin the final implementation, repeat the key experiments, verify ground truth and reachable postings, record hardware and cache conditions, and add a suitable external or flat-index baseline if claiming comparative superiority.
